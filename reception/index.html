Reception and critique¶

Home › Reception · sources rated in sources/EVALUATION.md

Everything in this file is evaluation, by reviewers, critics or (where marked) us. The summaries in chapters/ and concepts/ deliberately leave it out.

1. Publication history and audience¶

  • 2006 (FR). Published by Flammarion in October as a short, cheap book co-signed with the new association. It was one of four Stiegler books that year, alongside Mécréance et discrédit 2–3 and La Télécratie contre la démocratie, and it was the most programmatic of them. Reissued in Flammarion's Champs paperback series in 2008, which is the source of the "2008" date that some sites give (Babelio, catalogue records).
  • French reception is thin. It consists of a bookseller note (Charybde), a short web review that finds the book "peu accessible aux non-initiés" (Cherrier), and reader pages. A 2007 review appeared in Spirale. Ars Industrialis's own sessions (2005–07) were the book's main French context (context).
  • Early English circulation came before the translation, via tactical-media and net-art milieus (Holmes 2007), and via two sections translated by Stephen Barker in New Formations (2012) (Barker).
  • South African reception (2012). A pair of Afrikaans articles in Tydskrif vir Geesteswetenskappe: de Beer's enthusiastic review-article and Roodt's Arendtian critique of the manifestos (de Beer, Roodt).
  • 2014 English edition. Reviewed by Patrick Lyons in Critical Inquiry and Christian Lotz in Marx & Philosophy Review of Books (2015). (Lyons, Lotz).

2. Lines of critique¶

2.1 "Abstract diagnosis, empty remedy"¶

Lotz: the positive notion of spirit is expressed through "empty placeholders" ("battle against regression", "conquest of the best over the worst", p. 71). The only concrete example of cooperative knowledge tools is Wikipedia (p. 87). Lyons agrees that the book offers few concrete answers, though he reads it as a "philosophy of hope". Goodreads readers repeat the verdict: strong diagnosis, abstract remedies (Goodreads). - Possible response (labelled interpretation): the book does contain sectoral proposals. These are Ch. 2's "practical consequences" (pp. 91–94: specific instruments for research and education, new editorial functions, delinearized audiovisual objects, societies of authors and readers) and the 2010 Manifesto's seven policy planks (six in the web draft). What is missing is less concreteness than an account of political agency: who carries the programme against whom.

2.2 The Marxist objection: proletarianization without class¶

Lotz: control of labour "evaporates" into control of consumption. The production/consumption dialectic and the role of money and capital go unanalysed, and the framework is Kantian, Enlightenment and reformist social-democratic. Hutnyk (2012) and Manche (2021) generalize the point. Redefining proletarianization as loss of knowledge sidelines exploitation and accumulation. It yields "good capitalism vs bad capitalism", and the contributory economy risks self-exploitation and co-optation (Hutnyk, Manche). - Primary evidence relevant to the dispute: the book's own section titles ("Making the revolution of capitalism"; "The jumpstart"); Stiegler's 2005 call for an "enlightened capitalism" (Libération); the Tunis motion's "new spirit of capitalism"; and Stiegler in 2010 bracketing the question of whether the new model will still be capitalism (Lemmens interview). The reformist reading is textually well grounded. Whether reformism is a fault is a judgement of framework.

2.3 Statism and Europeanism¶

The manifestos and motion call for a "new form of public power" with industry, and for a European industrial policy (Beffa, Lisbon). Lemmens (2017) finds this statist, and doubts it can be squared with the decentralized contributory model (Lemmens). Lotz notes the "politics of decentralization" (p. 63) but sees no movement to carry it.

2.4 Spirit and its genealogy¶

  • Roodt (Arendtian): the 2005 Manifesto invokes Arendt's Life of the Mind, but by treating mind as libido and as a function of economic forces it loses Arendt's distinction between thinking and doing.
  • De Beer: embraces the spirit vocabulary in a quasi-spiritual, Heideggerian register.
  • Abbinnett: a Derridean reading. Spirit must pass through technical memory, against Beardsworth's "futural" reading (Abbinnett).
  • Rossouw: re-enchantment and the "new otium" as Christianity by other means (Rossouw).
  • Cohen: the Valéry genealogy; care as Foucauldian epimeleia (Cohen).

2.5 Media-theory reception¶

Hansen finds it "curious", even "perverse", that the programme seeks to save libidinal capitalism by re-enchanting it (Hansen). Lyons reads the 2014 book through the pharmakon. Lotz credits it with extending culture-industry critique from content to technological systems.

2.6 Decline narrative and empirical claims¶

Hutnyk reads Stiegler as a conservative "decline" theorist and contests his claims about television and children. The house glossary's claims about screens and synaptogenesis are asserted without discussion (glossary). Hutnyk also turns the pharmakon on Stiegler: "stupidity too can be pharmacological".

2.7 Style¶

Almost all readers note difficulty: "excessive use of technical terminology" (Lotz), associative prose and shorthand (Lyons), "peu accessible" (Cherrier). A minority call it "tonique" (Charybde).

3. The author's own revision¶

In The Neganthropocene (2018), Stiegler cites this book as his analysis of Simondon's associated milieus in the digital age. He also concedes that the algorithmic web has become "a systemically dissociating milieu, because it is hyper-proletarianizing". That partly withdraws the 2005–2010 hope that digital networks are intrinsically contributory (Ross file). By 2019 the programme had become a "contributory economy" against entropy, the Neganthropocene (MACBA podcast).

4. Afterlife¶

  • Ars Industrialis ran sessions, working groups, the Vocabulaire and the pharmakon.fr school. In 2020 it changed its statutes, becoming the "Association des Amis de la Génération Thunberg" (context).
  • "Available brain time" became a French classroom topic, via documentary rather than this book (fiche).
  • Recent readers find the book prescient about attention economies and AI (Goodreads).

6. What the book's own text says on these debates¶

Evaluation, labelled. The text of the book bears directly on several of the disputes above.

  • Reformism is explicit, with a hedge. Capitalism "to this day does not have a credible alternative" (Introduction, p. 6). The aim is to make "the revolution of a capitalism that is on the road to collapse" together with "revolutionary capitalists", likened to the French bourgeoisie of the late eighteenth century (p. 53). Public opinion should back industrial capitalism against financier capitalism (p. 7). This supports Lotz, Hutnyk and Manche on the reformist reading. Yet Stiegler also writes of a new spirit of capitalism "while waiting for the replacement of the latter by another form of society" (p. 37), and of no future "of capitalism, or a beyond of capitalism, which is certainly not eternal" (p. 60). His position is therefore post-capitalist in horizon and reformist in strategy, not simply pro-capitalist.
  • "Only Wikipedia" is too strong. Lotz says Wikipedia is the book's only example of cooperative knowledge instruments. Chapter 1 already rests its alternative on open-source software, Linux and the IP network as an associated milieu (pp. 36–37), and the 2010 Manifesto adds free software, smart grids and AMAP.
  • "Abstract remedy" is partly answered. Chapter 1 specifies the agents (a new European public power; enterprising citizens), the model (the economy of contribution) and the danger (financier capitalism and tele-cracy). Its political agency remains thin, as Lotz says: there is no account of how the public power is to be built against the interests that profit from dissociation, interests the chapter itself identifies (p. 40).
  • The labour objection. Lotz says labour control "evaporates". Chapter 1 does address labour: Taylorism as the grammatization of gesture (n. 38); de-industrialization as offshoring to "very poorly remunerated workers, if not… slaves" (p. 29); and the proletarianized producer turned consumer under Fordism (p. 35). But it subordinates exploitation of labour to the loss of knowledge and the exhaustion of libido, which is just what the Marxist critics object to.
  • The decline narrative. Chapter 1's evidence for "spiritual misery" is largely anecdotal and drawn from the press: a Nice crime, Skyblogs, the Durn and Brunerie cases, the November 2005 riots, an Inserm study on attention disorders (pp. 39, 57–58). This bears out Hutnyk's methodological complaint, though it is consistent with the book's genre as an engaged manifesto.
  • The pharmakon reading (Lyons) is supported by the 2006 text itself (pp. 47–51), not just by the 2010 Manifesto.
  • Lotz's page citations are accurate. His references to pp. 1–4, 6–7, 23–24, 33 ("enslaved spirit"), 57 (short-circuiting transindividuation) and 58 ("lost the feeling of existing") all match the text.
  • Property. The complete motion (p. 95) calls knowledge a common good that "does not belong to anyone" and accepts only temporary limits from intellectual property. That is a sharper challenge to property relations than the Marxist reviews allow. Ch. 2 still frames the aim as "the reconstitution of a capitalist future" (p. 67).
  • State and the US model. The motion praises American federal industrial strategy (the internet, the FCC, the State with universities and the military) as a model for Europe (pp. 98–99). This supports Lemmens's charge of statism, but it is a strategic, not a nationalist, statism.
  • Concreteness. Ch. 2 names specific projects (INA 1997, IRCAM, IRI, the 1989–92 computer-assisted-reading prototype, the 1996 Nord-Pas-de-Calais report). The "abstract remedy" charge fits the philosophical framing better than the policy content.
  • Hindsight. The judgement that Lisbon "will have been a total failure" (p. 91) may be a later revision for the English edition.

7. Open questions¶

These are open questions the sources leave undecided. They are posed neutrally and are not answered here:

  • Does the knowledge/information (negentropy/entropy) distinction hold up outside its thermodynamic metaphor?
  • Is "loss of knowledge" a deeper or a narrower definition of proletarianization than Marx's?
  • Can an "industrial politics of spirit" be carried by public power and industry, as the book proposes, or does it require a social movement, as its critics say?
  • How should the 2006 hope for digital "new spiritual devices" be read after platform capitalism and the author's own 2018 revision?
