A Motion Adopted by Ars Industrialis on the Eve of the Tunis Summit (pp. 95–100)¶

Home › Overview › Structure › Tunis motion · prev: ← Chapter 2

The text. pp. 95–100, with notes on p. 112. Note 1 says that the World Summit on the Information Society (WSIS) was held in Tunis on 16–18 November 2005, and that the motion "had been published in part" by Libération on 14 November 2005. The web version (source) is that partial text. The motion in the book is longer: it is numbered, sets out three conditions, and adds passages on intellectual property, Porto Alegre, a European research programme, the US Federal Communications Commission, Viviane Reding and the French government. The French edition prints the motion at FR p. 163 (Ch. 2, n. 6).

Abstract (L2)¶

Ars Industrialis's position for the UN summit is set out in one subheading, "The true stakes of the Tunis summit", followed by numbered points.

  • Knowledge, not information. The real stake of the "information society" is the growth of knowledge and intelligence, which the motion calls a common good of humanity. Intellectual property can at most justify temporary limits on that.
  • A missed opportunity. Tunis has missed the chance for a global debate. Society now faces spiritual misery under a consumerist capitalism of exhaustion and desublimation.
  • Spirit, not only religion. The need for spirit must not be met only by religion. The life of spirit happens in work, relationships and communications media. Underinvestment in the value of spirit has fed "regressive and radical spiritualisms".
  • Another summit. The motion calls for another summit, in the spirit of Porto Alegre but "upon a different basis": an industrial politics of technologies of spirit, led by a new form of public power. It has three conditions:
  • treat information technologies and the culture and audiovisual industries together;
  • make television an instrument of knowledge rather than of industrial populism;
  • use digital technology to reinvent television beyond the broadcast model of the 1930s.
  • Europe. Europe should build itself on a long-term public research and industrial programme for the knowledge and culture industries. It should not seek short-term returns, and should look to American federal precedent such as the internet, the FCC, and the state working with universities and the military.
  • Living standards. The standard of living is measured by "the life of its spirit", not by protein consumed.
  • Criticisms. The Beffa report is silent on technologies of spirit. The Lisbon strategy and Viviane Reding's advertising proposals go the wrong way. France needs a "courageous discourse".
  • Close. Europeans can believe again only in Europe's singularity since Greece: a public space of knowledge, now made of cognitive and cultural industries.

Core claims¶

  1. The stake of the "information society", also called "cognitive capitalism" or "society of knowledge", is the development of knowledge and intelligence. The future of capitalism "hangs upon a growth of intelligence and knowledge" (p. 95).
  2. Intellectual property is a key but problematic element: alienation, expropriation, hegemony. Knowledge "has always been defined as that which does not belong to anyone", a common good, so only temporary limits to repay investment are acceptable (p. 95).
  3. Tunis's solutions delay the public character of knowledge. A society of knowledge can only be constituted "in a new form of publicness", a public space serving a social organization better equipped "to discern, critique, improve, create, transmit, and receive forms of knowledge" for the whole world's population (p. 95).
  4. Tunis misses the moment to launch a global debate. It will not pose the principal question: the relations of information, knowledge, technology, industry and society as the stake of transforming contemporary capitalism, which "no longer produces anything but exhaustion, desublimation" and regression (p. 96).
  5. Faced with spiritual misery (Weber's disenchantment; Valéry and Arendt's "life of the spirit (mind)"), people feel a need for spirit. Situating spirit only in religion is a danger. The religious is recognized, but the life of spirit plays out in daily work, relationships and communications, especially television, mobile phones, the internet and the web (p. 96).
  6. Failure to reinvest in the value of spirit "appears to us to have led to the proliferation of increasingly more regressive and radical spiritualisms" (p. 96).
  7. The appeal is international, not merely French or European. It aims to mobilize collective global intelligence around another summit, on a different basis from Porto Alegre and not as a monopoly over intelligence (pp. 96–97).
  8. "Spirit" covers both "available brain time" and the works of spirit (Valéry, n. 2: Regards sur le monde actuel) (p. 97).
  9. Three conditions (p. 97): (1) do not separate the information society from the culture and audiovisual industries; (2) if the information society is to be a society of knowledge, TV must become an instrument of knowledge, not of debasement and industrial populism; (3) digital technologies allow a TV entirely different from the 1930s broadcasting model.
  10. Europe (p. 98): a public action plan on the European plane; a great research programme with large public funds not seeking short-term returns, unlike current European Commission programmes; fundamental research comparable to medicine and physics; public power socializing results with economic actors, as the FCC does in the US. A new public will connecting industry, institutions, artists, media, publishers, territorial collectives and NGOs. Spirit is now "the foremost industrial resource", so new fields of externalities and solvencies are needed.
  11. Habits must change profoundly, which requires a formation of individual and collective intelligence and spirit, a new spirit of capitalism and a renaissance. Living standards are not measured by protein consumed, whose excess leads to physiological ills, depression and demotivation (pp. 98–99).
  12. Chirac's commission to Jean-Louis Beffa (Saint-Gobain) for an industrial-policy report is welcome. No industrial civilization is possible without long-term public and private anticipation, and the US federal state and Asia pursue determined industrial politics; the socialization of the internet was one. But the Beffa report is "silent" on technologies of spirit (p. 99).
  13. Barroso's Lisbon strategy does not go this way. Viviane Reding's recommendation to increase advertising means a further drop in the audiovisual value of spirit and threatens the press's advertising revenue (p. 99).
  14. France must stop invoking cultural exception or diversity and frame an industrial politics of the cultural industries oriented to "an elevation of the standard of social life" against levelling and entropy. It cannot denounce "American sub-culture" while saying nothing of French television (pp. 99–100).
  15. Ministries (research, industry, culture, communication) must act on concrete objects: search engines (Jean-Noël Jeanneney), TV, broadband images, wi-fi. Together with the State and the EU, they must give Europeans hope "regarding their culture" (p. 100).
  16. Europeans can believe only in "the constitution of a public space of knowledge constituting a reason", reason as social motivation and "universal motif of spirit". Today that space is cognitive and cultural industries, the technologies of a new spirit of capitalism. The 20th-century model has left "a capitalism that has lost its spirit", arousing "mistrust, anxiety, and demotivation, that is, irrationality, if it is true that reason is in the first place a motif" (p. 100).

Notes¶

  • Context. WSIS's second phase was widely judged to have achieved little beyond the internet-governance compromise. Press-freedom groups criticized holding it in Ben Ali's Tunisia. Chapter 2 (p. 64) calls the summit "calamitous and caricatured" and notes it was "entrusted … to the director, Ben Ali". The motion itself ignores the venue issue.
  • Publication. The motion was adopted by Ars Industrialis; Libération printed part of it. The intellectual-property argument, the three conditions, Porto Alegre, the FCC model, Reding and the French government appear only in the book's full text.
  • Labelled interpretation: this is the most "official" register in the book, addressed to policy-makers in the Élysée, the government and the Commission. It contains the book's most explicit claim that knowledge is a common good limited only temporarily by property, which bears on the debate over whether Ars Industrialis challenges capitalist property relations (see reception.md).
