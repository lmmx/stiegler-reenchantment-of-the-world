## Abstract (L2)

This is the book's long policy essay on the "information society". It was written to shape Ars Industrialis's intervention at the 2005 UN summit in Tunis and is addressed to executives, CEOs and politicians (p. 70).

**History.** It opens with an industrial-political history of networks: the French Minitel, which followed the Nora–Minc report, and the Clinton–Gore internet. Both were "regal" acts of public industrial strategy. Minitel in fact opened "the age of associated milieus founded upon an industrial technology" (p. 61). Every new medium is a stage of **[grammatization](<https://lmmx.github.io/stiegler-reenchantment-of-the-world/pr-preview/pr-4/concepts/grammatization/index.md>)** and can produce either an **[associated milieu](<https://lmmx.github.io/stiegler-reenchantment-of-the-world/pr-preview/pr-4/concepts/associated-milieu/index.md>)** or an aggravation of control. Television in the hands of Giscard, Mitterrand and Berlusconi produced **tele-cracy**, "the perfect accomplishment of the dissociated milieu", which leads to populism (p. 62).

**Ideology.** The European Commission's ideology of the "information society", with its demand that everyone "adapt", fused with neoliberalism. **Adaptation**, as Medef's Seillière preaches it, is entropic: it demotivates and leads to dissociation and to the "loss of the spirit of capitalism" (p. 67). Patrick Le Lay's sale of "available human brain time" is its clearest expression. Such an economy makes knowledge into information. Information as a commodity is **entropic**, while knowledge is **negentropic** ([knowledge vs information](<https://lmmx.github.io/stiegler-reenchantment-of-the-world/pr-preview/pr-4/concepts/knowledge-vs-information/index.md>)).

**Memory and education.** Against Plato and Michel Serres, Stiegler holds that memory has always been exteriorized. The danger is exteriorization without re-interiorization, which leads to **disindividuation** and a new **"reign of ignorance"**. The school, built on alphabetic writing, is the model institution of re-interiorization. It is now in crisis before the program industries, because modern education never understood its own technical basis.

**Remedy.** The remedy is an industrial politics of **[technologies of spirit](<https://lmmx.github.io/stiegler-reenchantment-of-the-world/pr-preview/pr-4/concepts/technologies-of-spirit/index.md>)**: new instruments of knowledge for research and higher education, a new publishing industry, audiovisual objects that break up mass audiences, and cooperative "societies of authors and readers". **Wikipedia** and Web 2.0 show that these are possible. All this should be backed by a long-term European public power that fights financierization, and should be debated at "a new summit" (p. 93).

## Core claims

1. Big public industrial strategies, from Minitel after Nora–Minc (1978) to the Clinton–Gore internet (1993), are what open new technical epochs. France abandoned such strategy after Giscard; Mitterrand conceded a TV channel to Berlusconi (pp. 61–62).
2. Each network technology is a stage of grammatization whose social meaning outstrips its makers' intentions. It is revealed only through "economic, political, and social, but also spiritual, battles", the "epokhal double redoubling" (p. 62; n. 4).
3. Tele-cracy is the "perfect accomplishment" of the dissociated milieu and leads to populism. The internet is at once a rupture (an associated milieu) and a new stage of control (R technologies) (p. 62).
4. Decentralization under Mitterrand, plus the European Commission's takeover of research policy, fed a libertarian then neoliberal abandonment of public responsibility. Europe piggy-backed US industrial policy (pp. 63–64).
5. The EU's "information society" ideology produced the "calamitous and caricatured" Tunis summit, entrusted to Ben Ali, and an infrastructure favouring short-term market criteria for social change (p. 64).
6. Cognitive capitalism arises from four things (p. 65): diffusion of cognitive technologies, the service economy, externalized production controlled through marketing, and the convergence of cultural and cognitive technologies. Used as R technologies, these lower the value of spirit, which amounts to **desublimation**.
7. The stake is "spirit understood as the power to sublimate" and socialization as sublimation within a libidinal economy (p. 65). The appearance of technologies favourable to associated milieus makes a "reversal" possible.
8. The firm is the main obstacle to change, because it drives dissociated milieus. **Adaptation** is "deeply entropic and demotivating" (p. 67).
9. Only associated milieus let "the life of the spirit … blossom". Building them needs long-term action by "a new international public power" against financierization, for the sake of "a capitalist future" (p. 67).
10. Le Lay's "available human brain time" is "a remarkably clear and concise formulation of dissociation" (p. 67). It amounts to the systematic organization of the drop in the value of spirit and to "décervelage" (Ubu's brain-removal) (pp. 68–69).
11. Spirit cannot be "adapted". One learns a theorem by re-living its genesis (*methodos*), so controlling spirit destroys it (p. 69).
12. Against TINA ("there is no alternative", Chomsky): "there is no alternative to a change in the industrial model" (p. 69).
13. **Knowledge is critique**, and so it contradicts adaptation (p. 72). The losses of *savoir-faire* and *savoir-vivre* in recent decades are losses of individuation, singularity and desire (p. 72).
14. All knowledge needs instruments. The issue is instrumentalization, as with the old Paris bookshop sign "Instruments of thought". Control technologies can become technologies of individuation (pp. 73–74).
15. Hyperindustrialization is unavoidable. The real alternative is associated milieus, the "a priori condition of any society worthy of the name" (pp. 74–75). The common limit of all present crises is **dissociation** (p. 75).
16. Information as a commodity is entropic; knowledge is negentropic and "constitutes the time of existence" (p. 77). Lyotard and Portella are cited on knowledge produced to be sold (p. 78).
17. Against Serres: the exteriorization of memory is "the very origin of man". Memory is always already "collective and objective" (p. 79).
18. The threat is exteriorization without interiorization. A knowledge society that fails to organize the social appropriation of new memory technologies becomes a "reign of ignorance" (p. 80).
19. Individuation is psychic *and* collective, and knowledge exists only when shared. The convergence of TV with the IP network can reintroduce dissociation through "push" logic. "Pull" makes the receiver a sender (pp. 81–82).
20. "Knowledge management" is managerial, not cognitive. Investment in instruments of knowledge has been absent, giving disappointing e-learning, whereas **Wikipedia** shows the power of participatory associated milieus (pp. 83–87).
21. Memory is selection. An instrument of knowledge produces its own selection criteria, and an ecological crisis of spirit is possible (pp. 87–88).
22. Technoscience has abandoned the ontological project of knowledge. The school, once "the matrix … of society as an associated and sapid milieu", risks becoming an institution of dissociation (p. 89).
23. Education was never thought from originary exteriorization. Kant ignored the "prosthetic synthesis", so the crisis of spirit appears first as a **crisis of education** (pp. 89–90).
24. Practical consequences: instruments of knowledge must be specific, hierarchical and reversible. Two tasks follow, new instruments for research and a new editorial industry. Three conditions make this feasible (pp. 91–93).

## Argumentative moves

- **Industrial-political history as argument.** The chapter treats Minitel, TCP/IP and the Commission's information-society programmes as proof that technical epochs are *politically* opened or squandered.
- **Turning the adversary's text against him.** Seillière's preface and Le Lay's interview, "some dozen pages" apart in the same book, are read as a single logic of adaptation (p. 67).
- **Thermodynamic contrast.** Entropy (information, adaptation, dissociation) is set against negentropy (knowledge, association, individuation). This is the axis of the whole chapter.
- **Anti-Platonic theory of memory.** Hypomnesis is the condition of anamnesis (Phaedrus; Husserl's *Origin of Geometry*; Leroi-Gourhan; Goody).
- **Phenomenology of the instrument.** Mallarmé's "The Book, a spiritual instrument", Husserl's "spirit-invested objects", and the library as an associated milieu (pp. 84–86).
- **From philosophy to industrial specification.** The chapter ends with design principles and Stiegler's own projects at INA, IRCAM and Centre Pompidou's IRI.

| 61–64 | **From the "informatization of society" to the "society of information"** | The internet's worldwide growth dates from Clinton and Gore's 1993 decision to make it the spearhead of their politics. This was a public industrial strategy and a "regal gesture" repeating France's **Minitel**, which followed the **Nora–Minc** report via the DGT under Gérard Théry. Minitel socialized micro-computing (n. 1: the exhibitions *Les Immatériaux*, 1985, and *Mémoires du futur*, 1987) and "inaugurated the age of associated milieus founded upon an industrial technology", even if it began with the *messageries roses* (adult chat services). ARPANET was demilitarized, there was Berners-Lee's work at CERN, and broadband followed. France's last great industrial project was Giscard's. Mitterrand created a pay channel and conceded a channel to Berlusconi (with Ricardo Freccero). **Tele-cracy** is the "perfect accomplishment of the dissociated milieu" and leads to populism. The internet is both rupture and a new stage of control by R technologies. Like the alphabet, printing press, machine-tool and TV, it is a stage of **grammatization** that outstrips its actors' intentions, the "epokhal double redoubling" (n. 4). The internet is decentralized and terminal-agnostic. The French critique of "Jacobin" Minitel missed that Clinton–Gore were just as regal. Mitterrand's decentralization and the Commission's takeover of research policy produced a "double de-centering" and then libertarian and neoliberal ideology. Europe piggy-backed US policy despite its own labs (GSM). The Commission christened the "information society" and demanded "adaptation". The IRISI programme was read through e-commerce and tax exemptions; n. 5 refers to Stiegler's own 1996 report for Nord-Pas-de-Calais. Convergence, anticipated by Nora–Minc, became deregulation and privatization. The ideology led to the "calamitous and caricatured" Tunis summit, entrusted to Ben Ali, and to Boltanski–Chiapello's "remote control", favouring short-term market criteria for **meta-transformation**. |
| 64–66 | **From the "information society" to "societies of knowledge"—or, on the possibility of "re-enchanting the world"** | TCP/IP, digitization and convergence are the origin of "societies of knowledge". Tunis presented itself as transitional. The economy became hyperindustrial or "cognitive" through four factors: cognitive technologies; the becoming-tertiary of services; capitalism externalizing production to control "conceptual and behavioral apparatuses" by marketing; and the convergence of cultural and cognitive technologies into what ought to be **technologies of spirit**. As R technologies these lower the value of spirit, i.e. **desublimation** (Marcuse 1955; n. 9: "I do not share", even "frankly opposed"). The "Save Research" movement shows the stake is knowledge, not information. The stake is "spirit understood as the power to sublimate", socialization as sublimation in a libidinal economy, through **hypomnemata**. Technologies favourable to associated milieus make a "veritable reversal" possible, as Kessler wished in commenting on Medef's 2005 "re-enchanting the world". Kessler cited Blair's proposal to redirect CAP funds to cognitive technologies. Seillière's preface: the end of the West's "rente technologique" and the turn to human capital (n. 10). The question is geopolitical (India, China, Japan) and is also about re-enchanting the **European project**, not just firms steered by financier capitalism's short-term returns. |
| 66–70 | **Enterprises, public power, and industrial populism** | The firm drives dissociated milieus and is the main obstacle, against Medef's "head in the global, feet in the local". **Adaptation** is "deeply entropic and demotivating", the "abandonment of any vision of the future" (Seillière, ex-Medef and president of Marine Investment). It leads to dissociation, desocialization, disindividuation and demotivation, the loss of the spirit of capitalism (p. 67; n. 12). Only associated milieus let the life of spirit blossom. A **new international public power** of public and private actors must found a new industrial civilization and fight financierization for "a capitalist future". Seillière's adaptation leads to nihilism (Nietzsche). Le Lay's declaration, "some dozen pages" later in the same book under the title "TF1—The School of Reactivity", is a clear formulation of dissociation. Selling brain time makes "a total absence of thought" into a standardized product (p. 68; n. 13). Adaptation generalizes the availability of brains, including executives' own, under fatalism, which the Enlightenment fought. It breeds social and moral regression and suicidal extremes, and amounts to *décervelage*. Spirit cannot be adapted: one learns geometry by re-making the theorem (*methodos*), so control destroys spirit (p. 69). Chomsky's TINA: the reality is "no alternative to a change in the industrial model". The information society is a society of control by R technologies; the knowledge society is "the generalization of associated milieus". The dissociated model leads to **industrial populism**, ruinous for society and eventually for firms, and to "generalized debasement". Stiegler addresses executives and politicians in charge of society as public space and time, an "as-sociation" (p. 70). |
| 70–71 | **Changing the industrial paradigm** | Available brain time is neither knowledge nor information: Pavlovian conditioning makes judgement unavailable. It is the worst case of the **society of control** (Bradbury and Truffaut's *Fahrenheit 451*). Le Lay boasts that TF1 "knows" its clients after 24 hours, and calls *Star Academy* (n. 15: TF1's softer answer to M6's *Loft*, with Endemol) "controlled reactivity", worthy of Papa Ubu. A knowledge society requires producers, consumers, entrepreneurs and governors to rise "toward ever more refined forms of the life of the spirit", "a conquest of the best over the worst", a "battle against regression", through the creations of sublimation (p. 71). Seillière calls for social intelligence while prefacing Le Lay. These contradictions express industrial society's "intrinsic debility". Overcoming them means changing **axioms**: the UN's and Commission's prose, Tunis and Lisbon (n. 16) must move from **dissociated to associated milieus**. |
| 71–73 | **Information society, disenchantment, demotivation, and the control of forms of knowledge** | Medef can speak of "enchantment" because the economy is libidinal. Questions of spirit are first questions of *motives*, projected onto "the plane that enchants me" (n. 17, back to Ch. 1), i.e. psychic and collective individuation. Cognitive capitalism carries R technologies' dissociation to its climax, Weber's disenchantment. It is a stage of grammatization: control of knowledge of production and consumption by R&amp;D, design and marketing. This control makes knowledge adaptive and entropic, but **knowledge is "intrinsically contradictory to adaptation"**: it is critique (p. 72). Theses: (a) an entropic tendency against the "consubstantially negentropic structure of knowledge"; (b) losses of *savoir-faire* and *savoir-vivre* over a few decades; (c) these are losses of individuation, singularity and desire, the reality of the "information" society; (d) hence an industrial political economy of technologies of spirit, an **"industrial ecology of spirit"** (p. 73). |
| 73–77 | **The re-instrumentation of forms of knowledge and the future of hyperindustrial society** | All knowledge presupposes instruments, a "techno-logy". A Paris bookshop on Boulevard Bonne-Nouvelle had the sign "The instruments of thought". Printing and account books opened rational capitalism (Weber; Foucault's hypomnemata, n. 19), the Republic of Letters and Enlightenment, and critique "in Kant's sense". Today's digital instrumentation becomes **instrumentalization**, proletarianizing researchers and dissociating cognitive milieus. A right must be opposed to this fact. Control technologies, like Mesopotamian and Egyptian ones that became Greek hypomnemata, are the condition of individuation (p. 74). Hyperindustrialization is ineluctable, but if it rests on dissociation society will "come up against a wall". The alternative is "a refoundation of the human enterprise" as **civic enterprise** (n. 20: Gandois, Seillière and "some hope" from Laurence Parisot). Associated milieus are "the a priori condition of any society worthy of the name". Saturations (urban, physiological, cognitive, affective) mark the limits, in René Passet's sense (n. 22). Thesis: "the common limit to all of the limits is dissociation". Associated milieus offer a rebirth of knowledge and of industrial democracy, which has "collapsed into telecracy, … the Italian name for industrial populism" (p. 75). Only an elevation of collective intelligence (Husserl's "reactivation") can face demographic and energy limits: caloric use from 2,000–3,000 kcal for early humans to 150,000 (Europe) and 230,000 (US); population doubling times falling from 1,500 years to 37 (p. 76). Lévi-Strauss's pessimism (n. 24). Against Seillière's TINA "fatalism": think hyperindustrial knowledge in its necessity (a stage of grammatization) and its limit (dissociation by R technologies), toward "the lasting development of spirit" and a "digital instrumentality … of the intellect" (p. 77). |
| 77–79 | **Knowledge (*Savoir*) and information** | Information as commodity is "in its essence entropic": its value decreases with time (n. 25: Collins on the value of spirit and Walras). Knowledge is "in its essence negentropic". It intensifies (intensive magnitudes: Kant, Thom) and "constitutes the time of existence". Knowledge societies must open digital technology onto new associated milieus or remain fragile. Lyotard (1979): knowledge produced to be sold loses its use-value (n. 26–27). Portella: a "dangerous return to positive … knowledge", "know-how" without knowledge (n. 28). The information-commodity is entropic, knowledge negentropic: an internal contradiction caused by dissociation. Associated milieus are negentropic; dissociated societies homogenize. A critique is needed through analysis of grammatization: a "reversal of the entropic effects of information" (p. 79). No spirit without material milieus (Husserl, Leroi-Gourhan, Goody). The passage from mnemotechnical to mnemotechnological society is not, as Serres claims (n. 29), loss of memory for gain of knowledge. Exteriorization of memory is "the very origin of man" and the condition of knowledge, "collective and objective": grammatization. |
| 79–81 | **Knowledge and memory, or reign of ignorance?** | Plato's *Phaedrus* need not bind us. Living memory exceeds its "retentional finitude" by projecting itself outside, which allows transmission between generations (n. 31). The threat is exteriorization *without* correlative interiorization (= individuation), which induces "losses of knowledge". The school (Greek or modern) lets one adopt the past of knowledge as a future (n. 32). A knowledge society that ignores the need to organize **anamnesic capacity** through social appropriation of new hypomnesic apparatuses, by a politics of technologies of spirit implemented by "a new public power on the European level", would be a society of non-knowledge. That means neither Socratic ignorance nor Charles Lenay's "cognition of ignorance" (Compiègne), but "a new age of the reign of ignorance" (p. 80). Anamnesic capacity = knowledge's negentropy, its "revenance" (Euclid in Lobachevsky; Husserl's reactivation, n. 33). Memory is anamnesic insofar as it is an associated milieu (transindividuation); grammatization can dissociate (entropy) or associate (negentropy). Hence a politics at once of research instrumentation, industrial politics of knowledge instruments, transmission and education, editorial industries, and of television as a program industry, with research "subject to new types of obligations" (p. 81). |
| 81–83 | **The risk of disindividuation as the growth of ignorance rather than knowledge** | Informationalization threatens dissociation, a "society" of ignorance. It is also a chance for a qualitative leap (*saut*) of knowledge as psychic and collective individuation. Digitization integrates conception, production and consumption into one cognitive system, with TV and mass media as actors in all three. "Available brain times", starting with children's, are absorbed by dissociated milieus, while the IP network and Web 2.0 open possibilities of association. The question of knowledge societies is first that of **television**: its relation to digital media and public policy. The convergence of mass media with the IP network risks reintroducing dissociation through "push" (a new overflow valve, *déversoir*, of programmes). "Pull" is associative: the receiver becomes a sender and individuates himself while individuating the milieu (p. 82). Individuation is psychic *and* collective, so knowledge must be shared. Serres "belatedly" discovers Leroi-Gourhan (n. 34: losses of individuation in the West, *De la misère symbolique 1*). The risk is a weakening of individuation through conflict among the psychic, the collective and the machinic. Global financier capitalism blocks individuation and "has come undone", yet capitalism maintains its "perpetual returns". The task is re-appropriation through techno-logical equipment, i.e. "true societies of knowledge". |
| 83–87 | **Cognitive saturation and "knowledge management"** | "Cognitive overflow syndrome": information paralyses decision instead of becoming knowledge. **Knowledge management** is managerial ("cognitive" = decision-supporting), not scientific. Instruments of science and research are secondary effects of tertiary-sector software and services. Informatics went from scientific to managerial to bureaucratic. Nora–Minc saw a large public market, but the logic reversed to short-term profit, non-research applications and video games. The milieu could still "syncrystallize" (Simondon) a new associated milieu. **Mallarmé**: "The Book, a spiritual instrument" (n. 35), an antithesis in which spirit overflows its instrument. **Valéry** on the mortality of civilizations and the fragility of spirit. Frazer, the return of the dead; Pascal and Comte: humanity "composed more of the dead than of the living" (p. 84). **Husserl**'s "spirit-invested objects", book and spoon (n. 36), and the *Origin of Geometry*. The instruments of spirit are dead and need the living, and so need care (*therapeuma*). Digital hypomnemata are "new spiritual instruments". Google's library digitization and the BnF president's call for a "European Google" merely mimic search engines, whereas the BnF had posed the question of "spiritual navigation" (n. 37: Stiegler's 1989–92 computer-assisted-reading working group with Giffard and Gattegno; the AIS-Berger Levrault prototype cut by the Balladur government in 1993). The library is a place of production and genesis, of "spirit-invested objects", now digitized as a network (p. 85). Spirit is finite; it retains the trace through instruments, the "demiurgic factor" of technique, too often forgotten when instruments become industrial. That is the oblivion of the value of spirit (n. 39: Google; Brzezinski's "soft power"). Spirit has always placed itself "outside the living". The library is an associated milieu "par excellence"; the network can be too. To read is already to write; Jules Ferry made the letter the basis of the Republic. Without investment in new spiritual instruments, e-learning and educational software have been "disappointing, … deeply regressive" (p. 86). Meanwhile **Wikipedia**, "the cooperative and free encyclopedia", is an "extraordinary success" showing that the participatory logic of associated milieus is "an extremely new and powerful dynamic". Knowledge markets are insolvent in the short and medium term, so there is a case for mutualized public investment at European level. The insolvency concerns not just the two billion people without electricity but research labs' basic instruments (p. 87). |
| 87–89 | **Instruments of knowledge and the criteria of selection that they produce** | The alphabet is grammatization preceding all logic and grammar (n. 40: Auroux; the English bibliography misattributes his book). The third industrial revolution moves from mnemotechnical to mnemotechnological society, delegating cognitive functions to devices and absorbing or eliminating "the clerks" (n. 42). Memory, including information about the immediate present, becomes the object of vast **industries of memory and imagination** that separate producers from consumers. Memory is retention, hence **selection**, and its industrialization defines new criteria of selection (Google, "a poor example"). An instrument of knowledge "produces through its practice its own criteria of selection" as its practical rules. Such practices must be encouraged by public and private powers, not reduced to "cultural politics" but carried by an **industrial politics**: an *eco-logy* of the milieu of spirit. Spirit is originally exteriorized, so it is industrially exploitable. ICTs can reduce practical rules to procedural uses (R technologies), making "a major ecological crisis of spirit conceivable, and even probable". Future knowledge societies would subject the organization of information to the imperatives of knowledge, through "a politics of the regulation of new spiritual milieus" favouring their associative over their dissociative tendencies (p. 88). |
| 89–90 | **Being and becoming in "technoscience"** | Technoscience is "the industrial epoch of science" (Mallarmé's *Crise de vers*): a calling into question of being and of knowledge, an abandonment of knowledge's **ontological project**. The crisis of knowledge, of which schools are the theatre (n. 43: *Technics and Time 3*, ch. 4), comes from teaching that knowledge formalizes *being* when it explores *becoming*, with a new instrumentality not appropriated by schools. The school, "the matrix, *par excellence*, of the production of society as an associated and sapid milieu", is becoming an institution of dissociation that makes knowledge "nothing but a merchandise". Technoscience brings chronic instability, calculation at light speed, axiomatic and axiological crises (biology), and impossible transmission: teachers lag behind what pupils meet daily. Condorcet, Guizot and Ferry did not think from originary exteriorization, although the school consists in interiorizing techniques of exteriorization, "numbers and letters" (n. 44). |
| 90–91 | **The crisis of education** | The organization of knowledge rests on obscuring originary exteriorization. In Stiegler's reading of Kant's transcendental deduction, Kant ignored "the fourth synthesis of the transcendental imagination, or the prosthetic synthesis" (n. 45). So **program institutions** (education systems) are "completely divested" before **program industries**. Together with informational entropy and science's changing meaning, this makes a crisis of education necessary: "the ecological crisis of spirit translates itself in the first place as a crisis of education" (p. 90). Institutions that *produce* knowledge are in crisis too. A new instrumental chain of knowledge (digital instrumentality) is forming and can be oriented: the crisis "is not hopeless", but socialization as sublimation is threatened "at its very core". Public policy is required. Tunis should have debated this, but France and Europe, "left speechless by the very liberal 'Lisbon strategy'", stayed silent (p. 91). |
| 91–93 | **Practical consequences** | The chain of production and diffusion of knowledge currently works through "instrumental functions" of dissociated milieus (control, KM, R technologies). It must be **specific, hierarchical, and reversible**. (1) Instrumental functions of knowledge cannot be sub-domains of bureaucratic tools, document management, information systems and KM, as long as knowledge activities are not solvent markets and public power gives no long-term support. (2) Production instruments must be specified by users' qualifications and research practices, not by "broken models of use" from consumption (n. 46). (3) Diffusion instruments must implement the same primitive functions in simplified form, respecting the "communitization" of knowledge communities as associated milieus. Following Husserl, the recipient must be able to "retrace" a statement's genesis "techno-logically". An editorial industry should serve the institutions that prescribe programmes, which presupposes new production instruments defining norms of appropriation (a transductive relation between exteriorized knowledge and users' practices). **Two tasks**: new instruments for research and higher education; an editorial industry creating new functions for instruction and training. **Feasible** because (1) computer-assisted intellectual work (analysis, critique, argumentation, indexing, synthesis) is technically adequate; (2) program industries can make discretizable, delinearizable audiovisual temporal objects that de-massify audiences (INA hypermedia studio, 1997, n. 47; IRCAM music education with the Ministry; Centre Pompidou's IRI); (3) instruments applied at the source as supports of labour, cooperation and collective thought, "cooperative instruments of forms of knowledge", allow "societies of authors and readers" (n. 48) contrary to dominant business models and so "insolvent", "even if Web 2.0 is infrastructure that anticipates a new industrial model". **Close (p. 93):** these questions, unexamined at Tunis, should be the object of a new summit preceded by debate at EU level. Only then can a European project be built, which "can only be a European project of spirit: the spirit of Europe in the age of technologies of spirit". |

## What changed from the 2005 talk

Compared with the Colline talk of 24 Sept 2005 ([source](<https://lmmx.github.io/stiegler-reenchantment-of-the-world/pr-preview/pr-4/sources/primary-parallel-stiegler-colline-2005/index.md>)), the chapter keeps the section skeleton and the core theses: entropy/negentropy, reign of ignorance, crisis of education, practical consequences. It **adds**:

- a new opening history: Minitel, Nora–Minc, DGT/Théry, Clinton–Gore, Giscard, Mitterrand and Berlusconi, the European Commission, IRISI, the 1997 convergence green paper and Ben Ali;
- the vocabulary of **associated/dissociated milieus** throughout, plus "R technologies", "meta-transformation", "civic enterprise" and **tele-cracy** as "the Italian name for industrial populism";
- the four factors of cognitive capitalism, the "Save Research" movement and the Marcuse disagreement;
- Seillière and Marine Investment, Chomsky's TINA, Ubu's *décervelage*, and the geometry-theorem argument;
- demographic and energy limits (Passet, calories, population doubling times, Lévi-Strauss);
- the push/pull analysis of TV and IP convergence, and Web 2.0;
- the long meditation on Mallarmé, Valéry, Husserl's spirit-invested objects, the library, Google and the BnF;
- **Wikipedia**;
- the dismissal of the Lisbon strategy as a "total failure".

The procedural opening (§1) is gone, and the talk's §3 material on Medef/Kessler is moved into the second section.

## Notes

- **Labelled interpretation:** the entropy/negentropy distinction carries much of the argument and is asserted rather than derived. See [concepts/knowledge-vs-information.md](<https://lmmx.github.io/stiegler-reenchantment-of-the-world/pr-preview/pr-4/concepts/knowledge-vs-information/index.md>).
