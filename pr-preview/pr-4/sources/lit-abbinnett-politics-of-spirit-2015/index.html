Abbinnett, "The Politics of Spirit in Stiegler's Techno-Pharmacology" (2015)¶

A peer-reviewed article. Page numbers below are those of the accepted manuscript (pp. 1–13), not the journal's pagination. Abbinnett later expanded this material into The Thought of Bernard Stiegler (Routledge, 2018).

Condensed account¶

  • Aim (abstract, pp. 1–3). Abbinnett traces a notion of "reflexive freedom" from Technics and Time 1–2 to the pharmacology of What Makes Life Worth Living and For a New Critique of Political Economy. His thesis is that the pharmakon "sharpens the aporia" of technological society. Because human culture can be reduced neither to "the disorientated life of industrial populism" nor to idealist reflexivity, a "new politics of difference and autonomous desire" becomes possible.
  • Pharmakon (pp. 2–3). He starts from Derrida's "Plato's Pharmacy". Stiegler extends the pharmakon beyond Derrida's "orthographic economy" to the totality of human–technological interfaces. It becomes the antagonism between the toxic reduction of life to "capitalized desire" and forms of cathexis (love, spirit) made possible by techno-hybridisation. He quotes WMLWL p. 4: the pharmakon is "at once what enables care to be taken and that of which care must be taken".
  • Proletarianization and libidinal economy (pp. 3–4). The global economy is a regime of "technologically intensified desire". Inequality results from "pathologically disordered forms of libido", and the desire to consume is not confined to speculators or the bourgeoisie. Retaining "fidelity to Marx" means grasping the crisis of subjectivity, or spirit.
  • Spirit and its genealogy (pp. 7–11). It runs from originary technicity and epiphylogenesis (TT1, with epimetheia as anticipation of catastrophe "touched with the hope that human spirit may yet be sufficient") through the informatic reduction of memory (TT2) and Simondon's concretisation. Stiegler shares Derrida's suspicion (Of Spirit) of Heidegger's essentialist Geist, but finds a "Hegelian residue" in Derrida's orthographic culture. Abbinnett resists Beardsworth's Nietzschean, "remorselessly futural" reading of Stieglerian spirit ("Thinking Technicity", 1998). He holds that a politics of spirit must pass through epiphylogenetic, orthographic memory.
  • Conclusion (pp. 11–13). The fall in libidinal energy is "the determining limit of consumer capitalism". The same virtual technology that disperses the self is the condition for spirit's return as noesis. Sublimation (Freud's Project) is the basis of social cathexis. Stiegler's economy of contribution would free labour from "ratio" so that otium, "the labour of spirit", becomes "the global-universal good of prosthetic humanity" (citing WMLWL pp. 59–61 and NCPE pp. 65–66).

Short quotations (Abbinnett)¶

  • The pharmakon shows that culture cannot be reduced "either to the disorientated life of industrial populism, or to idealist notions of reflexivity" (abstract).
  • Global inequalities are "the outcome of pathologically disordered forms of libido" (p. 4).
  • "'otium' (the labour of spirit) emerges as the global-universal good of prosthetic humanity" (p. 13).

Notes for evaluation¶

  • Stance: Derridean and Heideggerian, sociologically framed. Sympathetic, but anticipates Marxist objections and tries to answer them. Valuable as a reading of spirit through Derrida's Of Spirit, which none of the other sources do. It also records the Beardsworth versus Abbinnett disagreement over the temporality of spirit (futural versus memorial).
  • Weaknesses: dense and sometimes loose. It glosses otium oddly as "the labour of spirit", whereas Stiegler and Ars Industrialis oppose otium to the negotium of labour. It conflates "biopolitical capitalism" with Stiegler's own "psychopower". Some typos (e.g. "Steigler").
  • Relevance to the book: it uses "industrial populism" as a key term but does not cite Réenchanter. It is useful for the spirit, pharmakon and otium slugs.
