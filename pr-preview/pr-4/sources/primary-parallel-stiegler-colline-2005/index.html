Stiegler, Ars Industrialis session of 24 September 2005 (Théâtre de la Colline)¶

Stiegler's talk to the Ars Industrialis session of 24 September 2005, and the precursor of Chapter 2. The printed chapter keeps the talk's section skeleton and core theses but is substantially expanded; see chapters/05 §"What changed from the 2005 talk". The talk is in 16 numbered sections. Sections 2 and 4–16 have headings that correspond one-to-one and in the same order to the 14 section headings of Chapter 2 in the English edition (pp. 61–94); compare STRUCTURE.md. Section 3 has no counterpart heading in the chapter. Its material on Medef/Kessler/Seillière reappears in the book's Introduction (pp. 2–3). The talk's purpose was to gather material for the motion before the Tunis WSIS (see primary-arsindustrialis-tunis-motion-2005.md). Christian Lotz's page references show material not in the talk, e.g. Wikipedia at p. 87 and judgement at pp. 70–72. So this talk must not be treated as identical to the chapter.

Condensed account (our paraphrase, by section)¶

  • §2 Informatization → information society. The "information society" grew out of e-commerce, disintermediation, tax avoidance and globalization's remote control (Boltanski & Chiapello). UNESCO's "knowledge societies" and "cognitive capitalism" name the same thing. Its roots are civilian networking from 1992 (TCP/IP; Clinton–Gore "information superhighways") and generalized digitization. That went far beyond the 1978 Nora–Minc report on the "informatization of society". In the 1990s audiovisual, IT and telecoms converged. The result is what Ars Industrialis calls technologies of spirit, which for now feed Valéry's "drop in the value of spirit", a desublimation (in a sense different from Marcuse's).
  • §3 Spirit as stake of struggle. Tunis is about spirit as the power to sublimate and socialization as sublimation, always within a libidinal economy and always through hypomnemata. Digitization has created new forms of them. The struggle is a reversal: what now lowers the value of spirit could raise it. Kessler glossed Medef's "re-enchantment" theme this way, citing Blair on moving CAP money to knowledge technologies. Seillière's preface to Les dirigeants face au changement speaks of human capital and the knowledge economy. The question is geopolitical: what would make a specifically European political economy of technologies of spirit, and could that re-enchant the European project?
  • §4 Enterprises and industrial populism. Against Medef, the firm is not the agent of this change. First, Seillière's "continuous adaptation" ends grand industrial strategy. Stiegler argues (Constituer l'Europe 2) that adaptationism is an entropic vicious circle that breeds demotivation. Industrial policy for technologies of spirit must be long-term and run by a new international public power with private actors. Second, the existing organization produces generalized debasement, a true industrial populism. The proof is that the same Seillière-prefaced book contains Patrick Le Lay's (TF1) statement that what TF1 sells Coca-Cola is "available human brain time".
  • §5 Change the axioms of the industrial paradigm. Brain time made available this way is the worst case of the society of control (Bradbury/Truffaut's Fahrenheit 451). Le Lay boasts of next-day audience data and calls Star Academy "controlled reactivity". A real knowledge society raises everyone toward refined forms of spirit through works of sublimation. The contradiction between Seillière's call for social intelligence and Le Lay's organized stultification requires a change of axioms. These changes should be the agenda of Tunis and of Barroso's Lisbon strategy.
  • §6 Disenchantment, demotivation, control of knowledge. Medef can talk of enchantment because the economy is libidinal. Spirit is a matter of motives (motivation) and so of psychic and collective individuation. Cultural/cognitive capitalism brings Weber's disenchantment to a head and is a stage of grammatization. It controls knowledge of production and of consumption through R&D, design and marketing. Theses: (a) a new instrumentation of research produces an entropic tendency against the negentropic nature of knowledge; (b) it coincides with losses of savoir-faire and savoir-vivre; (c) these are losses of individuation, singularity and desire; (d) an industrial ecology of spirit is needed.
  • §7 Instrumentation vs instrumentalization. All knowledge requires instrumentation, a "techno-logic", and industrialization of instruments is not denounced. Today's fact is instrumentalization, and a right must be opposed to that fact. Control technologies of societies of control (Burroughs/Deleuze) are the condition of future individuation. Mesopotamian and Egyptian control technologies became, as hypomnemata, the basis of Greek individuation and the citizen.
  • §8 Hyperindustrial society and renaissance of knowledge. Hyperindustrialization is unavoidable but has self-destructive limits. A renaissance of knowledge through instrumentation is the condition for crossing them, a "sustainable development of spirit". This requires digital instruments that are true technologies of intellect. Current models of industrial innovation are incompatible with the value of spirit.
  • §9 Knowledge vs information. Information as commodity is entropic: its value decays with time. Knowledge is negentropic: it keeps and intensifies its value, and it constitutes time. Lyotard (The Postmodern Condition): knowledge produced to be sold loses its use-value, "a doing without knowing". This is an internal contradiction of ICT-based knowledge societies. There is no spirit without a material milieu (Husserl's Origin of Geometry, Leroi-Gourhan, Goody). Against Michel Serres: externalization does not free us from memory to devote us to knowledge. Externalization of memory is the origin of the human and the condition of knowledge, and it is a process of grammatization.
  • §10 Memory, or the reign of ignorance? One need not follow Plato's Phaedrus in opposing living anamnesis to written hypomnesis. Living memory must project itself outside to overcome its retentional finitude. The real threat is externalization without correlative interiorization (= individuation). The school is the institution for appropriating the alphabetic hypomnesis. A knowledge society that fails to organize social appropriation of the new hypomnesic devices, through a European politics of technologies of spirit, would become a new reign of ignorance. "Anamnesic capacity" names knowledge's negentropy, its "revenance" (Husserl on reactivating the origin, as with Euclid in Lobachevsky).
  • §11 Disindividuation. Informationalization is both a threat and a chance. Digital convergence merges conception, production and consumption into one cognitive system, while children's "available brain time" is captured during the years meant for education. So the knowledge-society question is first about television and its relation to digital media, and it requires public policy. Individuation is always psychic and collective: knowledge is knowledge only when shared and public. The risk is not loss of memory but loss of individuation, an entropic collapse arising from unresolved conflict among the psychic, collective and machinic.
  • §12 Cognitive saturation and KM. "Cognitive overflow syndrome": information accumulates as unprocessable data instead of becoming knowledge. Knowledge-management tools are managerial ("cognitive" meaning decision-supporting), not scientific. Tools for teaching and research are spin-offs of the tertiary sector, and e-learning has been disappointing or regressive. Real knowledge markets are "insolvent", and public authorities have stopped underwriting them.
  • §13 Instruments of knowledge and their selection criteria. Following Auroux, the alphabet is grammatization preceding all logic and science. The third industrial revolution moves from mnemotechnical to mnemotechnological societies, where cognitive functions are delegated to machines and production absorbs the clerks. Memory, including information about the present, becomes the object of vast industries of memory and imagination separating producers from consumers. Memory is retention and therefore selection. Industrializing it means imposing new criteria of selection (Google's challenge to Europe). An instrument of knowledge produces its own selection criteria as its practical rules. An ecological crisis of spirit is possible because spirit is originally externalized and so exploitable. The aim is to subordinate the organization of information to the imperatives of knowledge.
  • §14 Being and becoming in technoscience. Technoscience constantly calls being into question and abandons knowledge's ontological project. Schools teach that knowledge formalizes what is, while knowledge now explores becoming. Axiomatic and axiological crises (e.g. in biology) make transmission nearly impossible, so teachers lag structurally behind what pupils meet daily. Condorcet, Guizot and Ferry did not think from originary externalization.
  • §15 Crisis of education. Stiegler's reading of Kant (Technics and Time 3) argues the transcendental deduction ignores a "fourth", prosthetic synthesis of imagination. Program institutions (education systems) are therefore helpless before program industries. The ecological crisis of spirit shows first as an educational crisis, and also as a crisis of knowledge-producing institutions. A new instrumental chain of knowledge (digital instrumentality) can and must be oriented by public policy.
  • §16 Practical consequences. Knowledge instruments must be specific, hierarchized and reversible, not sub-applications of office software, document management or KM. Production tools must suit users' qualifications. Dissemination tools should share their basic functions so that recipients can retrace the genesis of statements. A publishing industry for knowledge societies should serve the institutions that prescribe programs. Priorities: (1) new instruments for research and higher education; (2) new editorial functions for schools. This is feasible: computer-assisted intellectual work (annotation, criticism, argument, indexing, synthesis) is technically mature; discretizable and delinearizable audiovisual "temporal objects" can de-massify audiences (INA 1997 studio, IRCAM music education); hypertext enables societies of authors and readers, contrary to dominant business models.

Short quotations¶

  • Knowledge "is by essence negentropic", whereas commodified information "is by essence entropic" (§9, our translation).
  • The danger is a society of non-knowledge, "a new age of the reign of ignorance" (§10, our translation).

Notes for evaluation¶

  • Reliability as a proxy for Ch. 2: high for the argument skeleton, moderate for detail. The book version adds material. Lotz's citations show pp. 68 ("total absence of thought"; spirit as capacity to imagine alternatives), 70–72 (one's own and critical judgements), 75 ("collective intelligence") and 87 (Wikipedia). Those are not in this talk.
  • It contains a long Le Lay quotation sourced to Les dirigeants face au changement (Éditions du Huitième jour, 2004), p. 92. This is the standard citation for "temps de cerveau humain disponible".
  • Footnotes reference Stiegler's contemporaneous books: Constituer l'Europe 1–2 (2005), De la misère symbolique 1 (2004), Mécréance et discrédit 1–2 (2004–6), La technique et le temps 3 (2001). Ch. 2 condenses their arguments.
